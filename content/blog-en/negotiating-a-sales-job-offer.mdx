# Negotiating a Sales Job Offer: The Complete Guide

The irony is painful: salespeople make a living from negotiation, yet most of them are afraid to negotiate their own job offer. The same people who defend their price to a client without blinking accept the employer's first number with the words "thank you, that sounds great".

I have run hundreds of recruitment interviews - I have sat on the other side of that table many times. So let me tell you plainly how it looks from the perspective of the person making the offer.

## Why you should negotiate

The first offer is almost never the final word. Employers usually leave themselves some room, because they assume a good candidate will ask for it. If you don't negotiate, you don't just leave money on the table. You also send a signal that you don't fight for what's yours - and that is the last thing you want to communicate when applying for a role in which you're meant to fight for other people's revenue.

The good news: simply asking rarely ruins anything. A company that withdraws an offer because a candidate politely asked about the package has just shown you how it will treat you later.

## When to negotiate

Negotiate only once you have a written offer. Not before. Talking about money before the company has said "we want you" puts you in a position of weakness. After the offer, the situation flips - they have already invested in the decision and have the most to lose if you walk away.

That is why asking about salary in the first interview is a mistake. Your negotiating power grows with every stage you pass. Don't spend it at the start.

## How to set your target

Research the market before you sit down to talk: publicly available salary data, LinkedIn, conversations with recruiters and contacts in the industry. Set three numbers: the minimum you won't go below, a realistic target, and the aspiration you open with.

Without those numbers you negotiate by feel, and under pressure feel always drifts downwards. With them you have an anchor to return to when things get uncomfortable.

## How to open the negotiation

Don't apologise and don't explain yourself. Say calmly: "I'm very keen on this role. I have a question about the compensation package." Then give a specific number - always higher than the one you actually want to get, because a negotiation almost always moves down from the opening point.

And most importantly: once you've named the number, stay silent. Silence is the most underrated tool in negotiation. Whoever breaks it first usually gives ground - and that doesn't have to be you.

## What to negotiate beyond base salary

Base salary isn't everything, and sometimes it isn't even what's worth pushing hardest. Also on the table: the bonus and its terms, shares or options, the date of the first salary review, a training budget, flexibility in how you work. If a company can't move the base, it almost always can move something else.

A candidate who negotiates only one number boxes themselves into a corner. One who sees the whole package has far more room to leave the conversation satisfied.

## The most common mistakes

- **Giving a range instead of a number** - if you say "between X and Y", you'll hear X. Always.
- **Justifying your ask with your own costs** - a mortgage and rent are not an argument. Your value to the company is.
- **Accepting the first counteroffer without asking for more** - a counteroffer is rarely the ceiling.
- **Negotiating by email** - have difficult conversations by voice. Writing kills nuance and makes it easier to say no.

---

Negotiating your own offer is the same skill you're selling to the employer - just applied to yourself. If you can defend a price to a client, you can defend your own. Start with one rule: never accept the first number on the day you hear it.
